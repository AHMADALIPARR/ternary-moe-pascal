The lighthouse keeper woke before the gulls. He lit the stove, set the kettle on the iron ring, and climbed the hundred and twelve steps to the lamp room while the water warmed. From the gallery he could see the whole bay: the fishing boats sleeping at their moorings, the harbor wall with its row of lanterns, the long gray line of the breakwater, and beyond it the open sea, flat and quiet under a pale sky.

He wiped the great lens with a soft cloth, one panel at a time. The lens was older than the keeper and older than his father, who had kept the light before him. Each panel bent the flame into a single beam, and the beam turned once every ten seconds, so that a sailor far out on the water could count the flashes and know exactly which coast lay ahead.

When the kettle sang he went down again. He made tea, wrote the weather in the log, and read the barometer. The needle had fallen in the night. Wind from the south west, he wrote, sea calm, glass falling. Then he went outside to look at the sky, because the needle could only tell him so much and the sky told him the rest.

By noon the clouds had thickened. The boats came in early, one after another, their crews tying them twice to the rings in the harbor wall. A boy ran along the quay carrying a coil of rope. The baker closed her shutters. The keeper carried oil up the hundred and twelve steps, filled the reservoir, trimmed the wick, and checked the clockwork that turned the lens. He wound it until the weight hung at the top of its shaft.

The storm arrived in the late afternoon. Rain came sideways across the rocks and the wind leaned on the tower like a tired animal. Waves broke over the breakwater and ran white along the harbor wall. The keeper lit the lamp an hour before dusk, because in weather like this the dark comes early and a ship cannot wait for the proper time.

All night he climbed the stairs every two hours to wind the clockwork and watch the beam. Between climbs he sat by the stove and listened. A tower in a storm is never silent. The windows hum, the door knocks against its latch, and somewhere far below the sea keeps up a slow and heavy drumming against the stones.

A little after three in the morning he saw a light where no light should be. It was low on the water and moving slowly to the north, toward the reef. He watched it for a long minute. Then he took the signal lamp from its hook and flashed the warning, short and long, short and long, again and again, until his arm ached. For a while nothing changed. Then the light turned. It swung away from the reef, toward the deep channel, and slowly crept into the shelter of the bay.

In the morning the storm was gone. The sea was the color of slate and the air smelled of salt and wet stone. A small cargo boat lay at anchor inside the breakwater, its deck covered with broken crates. Its captain rowed ashore and climbed the path to the lighthouse. He did not say much. He shook the keeper's hand, set a bottle of good wine on the table, and stood for a moment looking up the stairs toward the lamp room.

The keeper wrote in the log. Wind dropped before dawn, sea moderate, glass rising. Vessel warned off the north reef at three in the morning, now safe at anchor. Then he climbed the hundred and twelve steps, put out the flame, and began to wipe the great lens with a soft cloth, one panel at a time.

Every evening the light came on again. Children in the town learned to count the flashes from their bedroom windows, one, two, three, up to ten, and then the beam came round again. Old sailors said they could find their way home with their eyes shut, just by feeling the rhythm of the light on their faces. The keeper said nothing about that. He only climbed the stairs, wound the clock, trimmed the wick and wrote the weather, and the light turned over the water, patient and steady, the way it always had.

Seasons passed. In spring the gulls nested on the ledges below the gallery and screamed at him whenever he stepped outside. In summer the tourists came with cameras and asked how many steps there were, and he told them, and they climbed them anyway. In autumn the fog rolled in from the sea and he sounded the horn every thirty seconds, long and low, until the sound seemed to live inside his bones. In winter the storms returned, and he lit the lamp early, and the boats came home.
